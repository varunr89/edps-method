## TLDR

Smith argues that labor is the only true and invariable measure of value, as it represents the real cost of acquiring any commodity through toil and trouble. Money serves merely as the nominal price, subject to fluctuations in the value of gold and silver, while labor remains constant in its sacrifice to the laborer. This distinction between real and nominal price has practical importance for long-term contracts like rents, where corn-based payments preserve value better than money payments.

---

## Key Terms

- **Real price**: The value of a commodity measured in the quantity of labor it can command or purchase; what something truly costs in terms of human toil and trouble
- **Nominal price**: The value of a commodity expressed in money (gold or silver); subject to fluctuations based on the supply of precious metals
- **Exchangeable value**: The power a commodity gives its owner to purchase other goods or command the labor of others
- **Standard of value**: The measure by which the worth of all commodities can be compared across time and place; Smith argues labor alone serves this function
- **Seignorage**: A duty or fee imposed upon the coinage of metals, which increases the value of coined metal above bullion

## Argument Structure

1. After division of labor, wealth depends not on one's own labor but on the quantity of others' labor one can command through exchange
2. Labor is the original purchase price of all things—gold and silver merely represent stored labor value
3. Money (gold and silver) varies in value based on mine productivity, making it an unreliable measure across time
4. Labor alone never varies in its value to the laborer—the same sacrifice of ease, liberty, and happiness is always required
5. Therefore, labor is the only universal and accurate standard for comparing values across different times and places, though money remains practical for everyday transactions

## Modern Application

The distinction between real and nominal prices directly parallels modern debates about inflation adjustment and purchasing power. When economists discuss "real wages" versus "nominal wages," they echo Smith's framework—a worker earning $50,000 in 2024 may be poorer in real terms than one earning $30,000 in 2000 if prices have risen faster than wages. Central banks like the Federal Reserve explicitly target inflation to maintain price stability, recognizing that fluctuating money values distort economic decisions. The 2021-2023 inflation surge demonstrated how nominal wage gains can mask declining real purchasing power, validating Smith's warning about money's unreliability as a value measure.
