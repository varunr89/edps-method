# Section 006: OF THE COMPONENT PART OF THE PRICE OF COMMODITIES

## TLDR

Smith argues that in primitive societies, labor alone determines exchange value, but in advanced societies, the price of every commodity resolves into three component parts: wages, profits, and rent. Profits are distinct from wages because they are proportional to capital employed rather than to labor performed. These three components—wages, profit, and rent—constitute the original sources of all revenue and exchangeable value in society.

---

## Key Terms

- **Stock**: Accumulated capital that can be employed to set workers in motion and generate profit through production
- **Profits of stock**: Returns to capital owners, regulated by the value of capital employed rather than by labor of inspection
- **Rent**: Payment to landlords for use of land, constituting a third component of price beyond wages and profit
- **Labour of inspection and direction**: Managerial work that some might confuse with the source of profits, but which Smith distinguishes as separate from capital returns
- **Derivative revenue**: Income like interest that derives from one of the three original sources (wages, profit, rent) rather than being original itself

---

## Argument Structure

1. In primitive societies without accumulated capital or private land, labor time alone determines exchange ratios between goods
2. Once stock accumulates, employers must receive profits beyond wages to justify risking their capital—profits proportional to capital, not labor
3. Once land becomes private property, landlords demand rent even for natural produce, adding a third component to price
4. Every commodity's price ultimately resolves into wages, profit, and rent—even when intermediate goods are involved, their prices trace back to these three
5. These three components are the original sources of all revenue; other forms like interest are derivative

---

## Modern Application

The gig economy debate directly echoes Smith's analysis of how wages, profit, and rent can be "confounded" when one person performs multiple roles. When an Uber driver uses their own car, they combine laborer (wages), capital owner (profit on vehicle), and sometimes even landlord functions—yet their earnings are reported as a single figure. This confusion matters for tax policy, labor law, and whether drivers should be classified as employees or contractors. Smith's framework helps explain why platform companies benefit from this ambiguity: they extract profit while workers bear capital costs typically borne by employers.

## Source Pointers

- **Key passage**: "The profits of stock, it may perhaps be thought, are only a different name for the wages of a particular sort of labour, the labour of inspection and direction. They are, however, altogether different, are regulated by quite different principles, and bear no proportion to the quantity, the hardship, or the ingenuity of this supposed labour of inspection and direction."
- **Best example**: Two manufactories with identical labor (20 workers at £15/year each) but different capital (£1,000 vs £7,300) yield vastly different profits (£100 vs £730), proving profits follow capital, not managerial labor
- **Strongest argument**: The demonstration that a principal clerk's wages for "inspection and direction" bear no regular proportion to the capital he oversees, while the owner's profits do—even when the owner performs no labor
