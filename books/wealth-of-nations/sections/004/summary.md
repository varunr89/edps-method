## TLDR

Smith argues that money emerged naturally to solve the "double coincidence of wants" problem inherent in barter systems, with metals becoming preferred due to their durability and divisibility. Coined money then developed to eliminate the inconveniences of weighing and assaying raw metal, though governments have historically debased currencies to pay debts. The chapter concludes by distinguishing "value in use" from "value in exchange," introducing the famous diamond-water paradox.

---

## Key Terms

- **Division of labour**: The specialization of workers in particular tasks, which creates surplus production requiring exchange
- **Double coincidence of wants**: The problem where barter requires each party to want exactly what the other offers (Smith's butcher/brewer/baker example)
- **Commodity money**: Goods like cattle, salt, or nails used as a medium of exchange before coined money
- **Value in use**: The utility or usefulness of an object to satisfy human needs
- **Value in exchange**: The purchasing power an object conveys; its ability to command other goods in trade

---

## Argument Structure

1. Division of labour makes individuals dependent on exchange, transforming society into a "commercial society" where everyone becomes a merchant
2. Barter creates friction because successful exchange requires mutual coincidence of wants between trading parties
3. Prudent individuals naturally stockpile universally-desired commodities to facilitate future exchanges, leading to commodity money
4. Metals prove superior as money due to durability and divisibility; coinage then solves weighing and assaying problems
5. Governments have historically debased coinage, defrauding creditors while benefiting debtors—a "universal revolution in fortunes"

---

## Modern Application

The 2008 financial crisis and subsequent quantitative easing programs echo Smith's warning about currency debasement—central banks expanding money supply effectively reduced the real value of existing currency holdings, benefiting debtors (including governments) at creditors' expense. Bitcoin's emergence in 2009 represents a modern attempt to solve the same problems Smith identified: its fixed supply addresses debasement concerns, while its divisibility (to eight decimal places) mirrors metals' advantage over cattle. The diamond-water paradox Smith introduces was only resolved in the 1870s by marginal utility theory, yet remains relevant in debates about pricing essential goods like insulin versus luxury items.
